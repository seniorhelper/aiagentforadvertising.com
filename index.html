Know what you are buying

Platform AI, a chatbot, or an advertising agent?

All three get called "AI." They do very different jobs, and the difference decides whether your Monday gets easier.

The chatbot-versus-agent split follows TechTarget: chatbots converse, agents act across systems to finish tasks. Our agents work the gaps between the walls, and they leave the bidding to the platforms.

What it takes off your plate

Nine ad jobs an agent can run while you sleep

Each one is a real, repetitive task that marketers, owners and small agencies do by hand every week. Pick one to start; most people do.

Budget pacing alerts

It checks spend against your monthly plan several times a day and pings you the moment a campaign is running hot, running dry, or quietly stopped serving.

A human approves: Any budget change. The alert is automatic; the fix is your call.

Search-term mining

It reads every new search term, groups the junk (jobs, free, DIY, wrong city), and drafts a negative keyword list with the reason next to each line.

A human approves: Which negatives go live, and at what match type.

Ad copy and creative variants

It drafts fresh headlines, descriptions and primary text in your voice, checked against character limits and your banned-claims list.

A human approves: Every word before it runs. Nothing publishes on its own.

Lead follow-up in seconds

New form fill, lead ad or missed call? It sends a friendly first touch by text or email right away and tells your team who to call.

A human approves: The templates it may send, and when a person takes over.

Weekly cross-platform reporting

Google, Meta, GA4 and your CRM in one plain-English recap: what moved, why it probably moved, and what it suggests next.

A human approves: The report before it goes to a client or a boss.

Landing page and tracking QA

It loads your ad destinations on a schedule, checks for broken pages, slow loads, missing tags and form errors, and flags them before the money burns.

A human approves: Nothing. It only reports, so there is nothing to approve.

Competitor ad monitoring

It keeps a running log of what competitors show in the public ad libraries and summarizes new offers and angles once a week.

A human approves: Whether any of it changes your plan.

Review and reputation replies

It drafts warm, specific replies to new Google reviews and nudges happy customers to leave a review right after the job wraps up.

A human approves: Every public reply, especially the unhappy ones.

Offline conversions and CRM sync

It matches closed jobs in your CRM back to the ad clicks that started them and prepares the upload, so the platforms learn which leads were real.

A human approves: The mapping of which CRM stages count as a conversion.

Human in the loop, in writing

The approval rules every agent ships with

Before we write a line of code we agree on three lists with you. They become part of the agent's configuration, not a promise in an email.

Does on its own

  • Read reports from your ad accounts and analytics
  • Send alerts to email, Slack or text
  • Draft reports, copy, replies and negative lists
  • Sort, label and log incoming leads and emails
  • Load landing pages and check tags

Drafts, then waits for a yes

  • Budget, bid and campaign status changes
  • New negative keywords and exclusions
  • Any ad text, image or offer before it runs
  • Public review replies and social comments
  • Client-facing reports and emails

Never touches

  • Billing, payment methods and account ownership
  • Deleting campaigns, ads or audiences
  • Prices, guarantees or legal claims you have not approved
  • Creating new ad accounts or launching new spend
  • Health or financial data without the right agreements

The honest market picture

Everyone is trying agents. Few are building them well.

Adoption is real and rising. So is disappointment, and the reason is usually the plumbing, not the AI.

Why most agents disappoint

In McKinsey's 2025 survey, 62% of organizations were experimenting with agents, yet just 23% had scaled an agentic system in any single function (McKinsey, The State of AI 2025). Gartner predicts a 10-to-1 ratio of AI agents to sellers by 2028, while under 40% of sellers expect to credit agents with a productivity gain; the leaders who clean up data, automation and UX before deploying are five times as likely to get ROI (Gartner press release, July 28, 2026).

How we build it

From a chore you dread to an agent on supervised duty

Four stages, each with a clear deliverable you can say no to.

  1. Audit

    A free 30-minute call where you show us the task. We map the steps, the tools, the logins and the places a mistake would cost money.

Least privilege

Every action logged

Your keys, your bills

No training on your data

The kill switch

Flip it and the agent stops running and cannot write anything. A second step pulls its access entirely. You can pull it from your phone, and so can we.

Sensitive industries

Health practices: patient information stays out of the agent unless every vendor in the chain has signed a BAA with you. Finance and lending: approvals on every message, and ads that follow the platform's special category rules.

Questions

AI agents for advertising, answered straight

What is an AI agent for advertising?

It is software that does a repeatable piece of ad work for you on a schedule or a trigger: it reads data from your ad accounts and tools, decides what needs attention using rules and a language model, and then either does a safe task itself (like sending an alert or drafting a report) or puts a suggested change in front of a person for approval.

Will an AI ad agent replace my ad manager or my agency?

No. We turn down projects that aim for that. The agent takes the clicking, copying and checking off a person's plate so they can spend their hours on strategy, offers and talking to customers. People still own the decisions that cost money or speak for the brand.

Can the agent change my budgets or bids by itself?

Only if you explicitly allow it, and by default we do not. Our agents suggest budget, bid and status changes and wait for a yes. If you later want small, capped actions to run on their own (for example pausing an ad that points to a broken page), we set that limit in writing and log every action.

How is this different from Google and Meta's own AI?

Platform AI such as Smart Bidding, Performance Max, Advantage+ and the new in-console advisors works inside one platform toward that platform's view of results. A custom agent works across your accounts, your CRM, your inbox and your spreadsheets, follows your business rules, and does the chores around the campaigns that the platforms do not touch.

Which ad platforms and tools can it connect to?

Most builds start with Google Ads, Meta (Facebook and Instagram), Google Analytics 4 and Google Search Console, plus the tools around them: Gmail or Outlook, Google Sheets or Excel, HubSpot, GoHighLevel, Slack, Twilio for texts, Calendly, Google Business Profile, Shopify and WordPress. If it has an official API or a reliable export, we can usually work with it.

Do I own the agent?

Yes. The code, the prompts, the rules and the documentation are yours, the API keys sit in accounts you control, and the usage bills go to you at cost. If you ever part ways with us, the agent keeps working and any developer can maintain it.

What does an advertising agent cost?

Our public price list is not set yet. The pricing section above shows the published 2026 market ranges. We quote each build as one fixed number once we have seen the workflow on a free audit call, and small one-job agents usually sit near the bottom of the market range.

How long does it take to build one?

It depends on how many systems it touches and how fast we can get access approved. A single-job agent that reads one ad account and sends alerts is a short build. Anything that writes to an ad account adds API access approval and a supervised pilot before it goes fully live. We give you a dated plan after the audit.
